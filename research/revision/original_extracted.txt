     Abstract— AI-generated Persian (Dari) text is often grammatically correct but still reads as AI-
generated – stiff phrasing, redundant precision, formulaic sentence patterns – regardless of whether the
underlying content is formal, casual, technical, or conversational. This paper addresses that broader
problem, humanization, rather than style transfer between registers: the goal is to rewrite AI-like Persian
into natural, human-sounding text while preserving its meaning, facts, and original tone, treating the
input’s register as a characteristic to be respected rather than a target to convert toward. We build and
describe a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) chatbot that retrieves semantically similar example
rewrites from a curated Persian parallel corpus and folds them into the prompt given to a locally hosted
Llama 3.1 8B model, coordinated through a modular pipeline (multilingual sentence embeddings,
ChromaDB for a single-pass similarity search, LangGraph for orchestration, FastAPI for the backend,
and Ollama for fully offline inference). We assembled a parallel corpus of 3,173 AI-generated/human-
written Persian sentence pairs; each pair also carries register and domain metadata (ten register
categories, eight recorded register transitions) that supports retrieval analysis but is not itself the
transformation target. Qualitative inspection of the generated output shows edits consistent with
genuine humanization – simplified verb constructions, reduced bureaucratic phrasing, softened register
– while numeric details, obligations, and factual content are preserved. We report retrieval
precision/recall, ROUGE (interpreted in the reverse of its usual direction, since low overlap here
indicates rewriting rather than copying), and output-length stability as characterizations of the
assembled system, and we are explicit that these are not a substitute for the comparison the paper’s
central claim actually requires: an LLM-only baseline against LLM+RAG, and a comparison across
retrieval depths (e.g., top-1 vs. the deployed default of top-4). That comparison, along with a held-out
retrieval evaluation that excludes self-retrieval, has not yet been run and is identified as the paper’s most
important direction for follow-up work rather than a claim made here. The current system also does not
include style-based retrieval filtering or a reranking stage; retrieval is a single embedding-similarity
search, and we discuss filtering/reranking only as candidate future extensions. We see the paper’s
contribution as distributed across a working, fine-tuning-free architecture, the accompanying dataset,
and an evaluation methodology honest about what has and has not yet been measured.

                                            I. Introduction
    Large language models have reshaped what’s possible in natural language processing over the past
few years, pushing the state of the art in machine translation, summarization, question answering, and
open-ended content generation. Persian has been slower to benefit. The language’s distinctive
formal/informal register split, its flexible word order, and the comparative scarcity of large, high-quality
Persian corpora all make fluent, natural-sounding Persian generation harder to achieve than the
equivalent task in English or other high-resource languages.

    That gap shows up in practice regardless of register: AI writing assistants are increasingly expected
to produce text that reads as though a person wrote it, not a system. Current language models generate
Persian that is grammatically sound but still often carries recognizably AI-like patterns – redundant
precision, formulaic sentence rhythm, nominalized bureaucratic constructions, hedged phrasing –
whether the underlying text is meant to be formal, casual, technical, or conversational. This is a broader
problem than formality alone: a casual AI-generated message can read as stilted in the same way a
formal one can, and the fix in either case is not to change which register the text uses but to make the
chosen register sound genuinely human. This paper refers to that broader problem as humanization, and
treats register (formal, casual, technical, and so on) as a property of the input to be respected during
rewriting, not as the thing being optimized.

    Recent research has shown that Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) improves language
generation by enriching the prompting process with semantically relevant examples retrieved from
external knowledge sources [1]–[3]. Instead of depending exclusively on the internal parameters of a
language model, RAG retrieves contextually similar information before generation, enabling more
accurate and context-aware responses. At the same time, the emergence of in-context learning has
demonstrated that Large Language Models can perform complex language tasks effectively without
repeated model fine-tuning [4], [5]. These developments provide an efficient foundation for designing
practical Persian language applications.

     Several previous studies have investigated Persian text style transfer, formality transformation, and
text normalization [5]–[7]. This existing work is closely related but narrower in scope than the problem
addressed here: it targets a specific register transition (typically informal-to-formal or the reverse) as
the object of study, whereas this paper treats register as something to be preserved and targets the more
general symptom of AI-like phrasing regardless of register. Most of this prior work also relies on
supervised learning and fine-tuned language models that require large annotated datasets, repeated
training, and substantial computational resources – requirements that increase development complexity
and reduce accessibility for educational institutions and individual developers, independent of the style-
transfer-versus-humanization distinction.

    To address these limitations, this paper investigates a specific question: can retrieval-augmented,
in-context prompting – without any fine-tuning – make AI-generated Persian text read as natural,
human-written text, while preserving its meaning, facts, and original register? Rather than modifying
the language model’s parameters, the proposed system retrieves semantically similar AI-
generated/human-written text pairs from a curated parallel dataset and incorporates them into the
prompt to guide text generation. Semantic retrieval is performed using multilingual sentence
embeddings and ChromaDB, while LangGraph manages the complete workflow from retrieval to
response generation. The language-generation component is implemented using the Llama 3.1 8B
model deployed locally through Ollama, enabling the entire system to operate without cloud-based AI
services.

    The proposed architecture adopts a modular design consisting of separate layers for data

architecture.

    This paper’s contributions span four distinct kinds of work, which we separate deliberately rather
than presenting as a single undifferentiated list, and which we do not conflate with each other – in
particular, integrating existing tools such as FastAPI, LangGraph, and ChromaDB is engineering work,
not a research finding, and is labeled as such below:

  • Research contribution: qualitative evidence, drawn from inspection of generated output, that
    retrieval-augmented in-context prompting can produce consistent, linguistically interpretable
    humanization edits – reduced bureaucratic phrasing, simplified verb constructions, softened
    register – without altering propositional content or the input’s original register. We describe this
    as preliminary: a controlled comparison against a no-retrieval baseline, which would isolate
    retrieval’s causal contribution, is identified as the paper’s most important open question (Section
    IV-F) rather than a claim settled here.
  • Dataset contribution: a Persian parallel corpus of 3,173 AI-generated/human-written sentence

    recorded register transitions) that supports retrieval and post-hoc analysis without being the
    primary training or evaluation target.
  • Engineering contribution: a modular, fine-tuning-free RAG architecture – multilingual sentence
    embeddings, ChromaDB, LangGraph, FastAPI, Ollama, and Llama 3.1 8B integrated into a single
    offline pipeline – that is straightforward to reproduce, extend, or swap components within.
  • Evaluation contribution: a reframing of overlap-based metrics for this task – since humanization
    is expected to reword rather than reproduce its input, we argue (and empirically confirm on our
    data) that a low ROUGE score, not a high one, is the signal of successful rewriting – paired with
    an explicit account of what the current evaluation does not yet establish, including a concrete
    experimental design (no-retrieval baseline, held-out retrieval evaluation, retrieval-depth
    comparison) for the follow-up work needed to confirm the research contribution above.

                                        II. Related Work
     Recent research in Natural Language Processing (NLP) has significantly advanced text generation,
text style transfer, and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). These developments have created new
opportunities for generating fluent and context-aware text using Large Language Models (LLMs).
However, research specifically focused on Persian text humanization remains limited, particularly in
approaches that combine semantic retrieval with prompt-based language generation.

    Text style transfer (TST), the broader task family to which Persian text humanization belongs, has
evolved considerably over the past decade. Early neural approaches relied on the sequence-to-sequence
(seq2seq) architecture [8], which compresses an input sentence into a fixed vector before decoding it


                                                   3
into a new form; this design, however, suffers from information loss on longer inputs and treats all
source tokens as equally important. To reduce their dependence on large parallel corpora, several works
reframed style transfer without requiring paired supervision: the Delete-Retrieve-Generate framework
[9] separates style-bearing segments from content and substitutes them individually, while back-
translation approaches [10] disentangle style and content implicitly by translating a sentence into an
intermediate language and back, injecting the target style during the return translation. These techniques
illustrate a broader trend in the TST literature: substituting expensive parallel supervision with retrieval,
substitution, or self-supervised proxies – a philosophy that directly motivates the retrieval-based, fine-
tuning-free approach adopted in this work.

    One of the most relevant studies is Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP
Tasks [1], which introduced the RAG framework by combining neural retrieval with sequence
generation models. The study demonstrated that retrieving relevant contextual information before text
generation improves factual consistency and response quality. This work established the theoretical
foundation for many modern retrieval-enhanced language generation systems.

     The emergence of Transformer-based language models further accelerated progress in text
generation. Vaswani et al. [11] introduced the Transformer architecture, which replaced recurrent neural
networks with self-attention mechanisms and became the foundation of modern LLMs. Later, Brown
et al. [4] demonstrated that large language models are capable of solving diverse language tasks through
in-context learning, reducing the dependence on task-specific model training.

    Recent open-source language models have also contributed to practical AI system development.
The Llama 3 model family [12] provides strong multilingual language generation capabilities while
allowing local deployment through frameworks such as Ollama. Local execution improves data privacy,
reduces operational costs, and enables offline AI applications without relying on commercial cloud
services.

     Alongside advances in generation, dense retrieval methods such as Dense Passage Retrieval [13]
have shown that learned vector representations can outperform traditional sparse retrieval for selecting
relevant context, forming the conceptual retrieval backbone that RAG-based systems, including the one
proposed here, build upon. Complementary work on neural passage re-ranking [14] has further shown
that a lightweight re-ranking stage over an initial retrieved set can improve the relevance of the context
passed to a downstream language model; we discuss this line of work as motivation for future extensions
to the retrieval module (Section IV-F), since the system described here performs a single embedding-
similarity search and does not currently include a re-ranking stage.

     Several studies have specifically investigated Persian language processing. Tajalli et al. [7]
introduced a parallel Persian corpus for informal-to-formal text transformation, providing an important
resource for Persian style transfer research. Building on a similar resource, Falakaflaki and Shamsfard
[6] proposed Fa-BERT2BERT, a Transformer-based model for Persian formality style transfer trained
with supervised consistency learning; notably, they also showed that conventional overlap-based
metrics such as BLEU and ROUGE can be misleading for this task, since a model that merely copies
its input can still score deceptively well when many words share identical formal and informal forms –
an observation that directly informs how this paper interprets its own ROUGE results in Section IV.
Separately, Rasooli et al. [15] addressed Persian text normalization by framing standardization of
colloquial Persian as a sequence-to-sequence translation problem, using synthetically generated parallel
data to convert informal text into its standard written form. Although these methods achieved
encouraging results, they primarily focus on supervised training and require large annotated datasets
together with computationally expensive fine-tuning.


                                                     4
    For representing Persian text itself, monolingual pretrained encoders such as ParsBERT [16] have
demonstrated strong performance on Persian-specific NLP tasks. In contrast, the proposed system relies
on multilingual sentence embeddings for retrieval, trading some language-specific optimization for the
flexibility of a shared multilingual vector space.

    More recently, several survey papers have highlighted the advantages of Retrieval-Augmented
Generation for Large Language Models [2], [3]. These studies conclude that semantic retrieval
improves contextual relevance, reduces hallucination, and enables language models to generate more
accurate responses without modifying the underlying model parameters. These characteristics make
RAG particularly suitable for languages with relatively limited training resources, including Persian.

    Despite these advances, an important research gap remains. Most existing Persian studies
emphasize text normalization or formality transformation, whereas relatively little attention has been
given to humanizing AI-generated Persian text using Retrieval-Augmented Generation. Furthermore,
many existing approaches rely on cloud-based AI services or computationally expensive fine-tuning,
limiting their practical deployment in educational and research environments.

     To address these limitations, the present study proposes a modular Persian text humanization
chatbot based on Retrieval-Augmented Generation. The proposed architecture combines multilingual
sentence embeddings, ChromaDB, LangGraph, FastAPI, and a locally deployed Llama 3.1 8B language
model to generate more natural Persian text while preserving semantic meaning. Unlike previous
Persian approaches, the proposed system performs semantic retrieval during inference instead of
retraining the language model, providing a lightweight, privacy-preserving, and extensible solution.

                                    III. Proposed Methodology
    This section describes the methodology adopted for designing and implementing the proposed
Persian text humanization chatbot. The system is built upon a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
architecture that combines semantic retrieval with a Large Language Model (LLM) to generate more
natural Persian text while preserving the semantic meaning of the original input. Instead of modifying




                                                   5
the language model through fine-tuning, the proposed approach retrieves semantically relevant
examples from a Persian parallel dataset and uses them to guide text generation.

    The proposed methodology consists of five main components: the overall system architecture, the
Persian parallel dataset, the semantic retrieval module, the language generation workflow, and the
backend implementation.

A. Overall System Architecture
    The proposed chatbot follows a modular architecture composed of four logical layers: the Data
Layer, Retrieval Layer, Processing Layer, and Presentation Layer. This layered design separates the
responsibilities of each component, improving maintainability and simplifying future system
extensions.

    The Data Layer stores the Persian parallel dataset together with the vector database. The Retrieval
Layer performs semantic search by converting input text into vector embeddings and retrieving the most
relevant examples from ChromaDB. The Processing Layer executes the Retrieval-Augmented
Generation workflow through LangGraph and communicates with the Large Language Model to
generate the final response. Finally, the Presentation Layer provides interaction between users and the
backend services through a web-based interface.

    Fig. 1 illustrates the overall architecture of the proposed Persian text humanization chatbot. The
diagram groups components into the four layers introduced above and traces the runtime data flow
across them (numbered steps 1–6): a request enters through the Presentation Layer and is forwarded to
the Processing Layer’s LangGraph orchestrator, which passes the original input to the Retrieval Layer;
there it is embedded and matched against the Data Layer’s ChromaDB index to obtain the top-k
examples. As highlighted in the figure, the retrieved examples and the original user input are both routed
into Prompt Construction, where they are combined into a single prompt before generation – the point
at which retrieval and the original input actually merge, prior to being sent to the Llama 3.1 8B model.

B. Persian Parallel Dataset
     To support semantic retrieval, a Persian parallel dataset was prepared whose core pairing is an AI-
generated (or AI-like) Persian input matched with a natural, human-written rewrite that preserves the
same meaning. Each record additionally carries register and domain metadata – a style label and a
recorded register transition – describing the pair, but this metadata is not the transformation target: the
system’s objective is to produce natural, human-sounding text appropriate to each input’s own register,
not to move text toward any particular register. The metadata exists to support retrieval analysis (Section
IV) and to let the corpus be filtered or audited by register, not to define what “success” means for a
given rewrite. Before indexing, the dataset was reviewed, cleaned, and organized to improve retrieval
quality and ensure consistency among the recorded registers.

    In its final form, the dataset comprises 3,173 AI-generated/human-written text pairs. Each pair is
labeled with one of ten register categories and one of eight recorded register transitions (e.g.,
formal_to_casual); these labels describe the composition of the source data collected for the corpus
rather than a target distribution the system is designed to produce, and their composition is summarized
quantitatively in Fig. 4 (Section IV).

    The dataset serves as the primary knowledge source for the Retrieval-Augmented Generation
process. During inference, semantically similar examples are retrieved from this dataset and



                                                       7
incorporated into the language model prompt; retrieval is based purely on semantic similarity between
the user’s input and the indexed AI-generated texts, without filtering or biasing by the register metadata.

C. Semantic Retrieval Module
     The semantic retrieval module is responsible for identifying examples that are most similar to the
user’s input, following the dense retrieval paradigm [13]. First, the input Persian text is transformed
into a dense vector representation using a multilingual sentence embedding model [17], itself an
extension of the Sentence-BERT architecture [18] to a shared multilingual space via knowledge
distillation. The embedding process can be represented as

                                                  q = f (x)
   where x denotes the input text, f(·) represents the embedding model, and q is the semantic vector
generated for retrieval.

     The generated embedding is compared with vectors stored in ChromaDB through cosine-similarity
search, and the top-k closest matches are returned in similarity order. In the deployed system, k = 4 is
used as a fixed default (Fig. 1); this value was not tuned against an evaluation metric and is not claimed
to be optimal. It is important to be precise about what this step does and does not do: retrieval consists
of a single embedding-similarity search, with no separate re-ranking model applied to reorder the
candidates afterward, and no filtering by the register metadata described in Section III-B – a retrieved
example’s register match to the query is an emergent consequence of semantic similarity, not an explicit
retrieval constraint. Adding a re-ranking stage or register-aware filtering is discussed as a candidate
future extension in Section IV-F, not as a component of the current system. The retrieved examples are
prepared for prompt construction in this similarity order; this retrieval process provides contextual
guidance for the language model without modifying its internal parameters.

D. Retrieval-Augmented Generation Workflow
    After semantic retrieval, the retrieved examples are combined with the user’s input to construct the
final prompt provided to the language model. The complete processing pipeline is implemented using
LangGraph [19], an extension of the LangChain framework [20] for building stateful, graph-structured
agent workflows, which coordinates the execution of individual processing stages.

    The workflow consists of two primary nodes. The first node performs semantic retrieval from the
vector database, while the second node generates the final humanized Persian text using the retrieved
context and the original user input. A shared workflow state enables efficient communication between
processing stages.




                                                      8
E. Language Model Integration
     The proposed system employs the Llama 3.1 8B language model executed locally through Ollama
[21]. Instead of applying computationally expensive fine-tuning, the retrieved examples are
incorporated into the prompt to exploit the in-context learning capability of the language model [4].
This strategy enables the chatbot to generate fluent Persian text while maintaining the semantic meaning
of the original input.

    Local deployment also improves data privacy because all processing is performed within the local
environment without transmitting user data to external cloud services.

F. Backend and User Interface
   FastAPI [22] serves as the communication layer between the user interface and the processing
modules. User requests are received through RESTful endpoints, forwarded to the LangGraph
workflow, and the generated responses are returned to the interface.

    A Persian right-to-left web interface was developed to facilitate user interaction. The interface
allows users to enter Persian text, optionally indicate a preferred output register, submit the request, and
receive the generated humanized output. This register preference is a user-facing option that biases the
prompt sent to the language model; it is not a separate model component, and the system’s default
behavior – and its primary objective – is to humanize the input while preserving its own original register,
not to convert it toward a different one. The interface was designed with simplicity and usability in
mind while maintaining compatibility with the modular backend architecture.


                       IV. Implementation and Experimental Results
    This section presents the implementation details and experimental results of the proposed Persian
text humanization chatbot. The objective of the implementation was to verify the effectiveness of the
proposed Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) architecture and to evaluate its ability to generate
more natural Persian text while preserving the semantic meaning of the original input.

A. Implementation Environment
    The proposed system was implemented using Python as the primary programming language. The
backend services were developed using FastAPI, while LangGraph was employed to orchestrate the
Retrieval-Augmented Generation workflow. ChromaDB [23] was used as the vector database for
semantic retrieval, and multilingual sentence embeddings were applied to convert Persian text into
dense vector representations [17].

D. Quantitative Evaluation
     1) Evaluation Metrics: To quantitatively assess the generated outputs, the ROUGE evaluation
metric [24] was employed. ROUGE measures the lexical overlap between the generated text and the
corresponding reference text and is widely used for evaluating text generation and text rewriting
systems. As noted by Falakaflaki and Shamsfard [6] in the context of Persian formality style transfer,
however, overlap-based metrics such as ROUGE and BLEU can be misleading for this class of task:
because many Persian words retain an identical surface form across styles and registers, a system that
performs little to no rewriting can still obtain a deceptively high overlap score. This work therefore
interprets ROUGE in the opposite direction from typical text-generation evaluation – since
humanization is expected to substantially reword the input rather than reproduce it, a comparatively low
overlap score is the desired outcome, while an unusually high score would instead signal insufficient
stylistic transformation. Table V reports the ROUGE scores under this interpretation, and Fig. 4 extends
the evaluation with a broader quantitative picture of the dataset, the retrieval module, and the length
characteristics of the generated text.

     A note on what this evaluation does and does not show is warranted before presenting the numbers.
The statistics below characterize the assembled parallel corpus and the retrieval module’s behavior over
it – they are not the output of a controlled experiment comparing the full RAG pipeline against a no-
retrieval baseline. Specifically, the comparison that would actually justify a claim like “RAG improves
humanization” has three parts, none of which has yet been run: (1) the same Llama 3.1 8B model
prompted without any retrieved examples, as a baseline; (2) the full RAG pipeline as deployed; and (3)
a comparison across retrieval depths, such as top-1 versus the deployed default of top-4, to check
whether the amount of retrieved context matters. In addition, it has not been confirmed whether the
queries used to compute the retrieval statistics below were held out from the indexed corpus – if a
query’s nearest neighbor in ChromaDB is itself (because the same example was both indexed and
queried), the reported retrieval precision would be optimistic relative to a genuinely unseen input. Both
gaps are treated in full in Section IV-F, where they are listed as required experiments, not as results this
paper claims to have. Until they are run, the findings below should be read as a characterization of the
system’s components on the assembled data, not as evidence that retrieval improves the end-to-end
humanization result.

     2) Results: Consistent with the low-overlap-as-success interpretation established above, all five
ROUGE variants fall well below 0.02, indicating that the generated outputs share very little surface-
level wording or sequence structure with their AI-generated inputs. This confirms that the humanization
process performs substantial lexical and structural rewriting rather than trivially reproducing the input,
which would have produced ROUGE scores near 1.0. The lower ROUGE-2 F1 score relative to
ROUGE-1 F1 further shows that even short, two-word phrases are rarely preserved verbatim, indicating
that rewriting occurs at the phrase level and not merely through isolated word substitutions.

E. Discussion
     The pattern in the qualitative examples (Section IV-C) is worth dwelling on, because it suggests
why retrieval-augmented prompting might work for this task even without a controlled comparison yet
in hand. The edits the model makes – collapsing nominalized bureaucratic constructions into direct
verbs, softening AI-typical hyper-formal markers, simplifying auxiliary verb chains – are exactly the
kind of local, example-driven substitutions that in-context examples are well suited to teach a language
model, as opposed to structural or semantic changes that would more plausibly require parameter
updates. This is consistent with the broader in-context learning literature [4], which finds that LLMs
are comparatively good at pattern-matching a demonstrated transformation and comparatively worse at
generalizing far beyond it. If that account is right, retrieval-augmented prompting should be well
matched to within-register naturalization specifically – rewriting stiff phrasing into fluent phrasing
without changing the register – and less reliable for humanization tasks that require restructuring
meaning rather than re-lexicalizing it – idiomatic expressions and culturally loaded phrasing, the exact
failure mode Falakaflaki and Shamsfard [6] report for their own, fine-tuned model, would be a
reasonable place to expect this approach to struggle too, since no retrieved example can substitute for
genuine cultural or pragmatic understanding.

    The retrieval-quality curve (Fig. 4c) offers a second, more cautionary reading. Precision@k and
Recall@k hover around 0.50 even at k = 1, meaning that in roughly half of cases, the single most similar
example in the corpus does not carry the query’s register label. Given that casual and formal_to_casual
together dominate the corpus (Fig. 4a–b), this is most plausibly a symptom of the class imbalance
documented there rather than a weakness of the embedding model itself: for underrepresented registers
such as humorous or calm, the nearest neighbors in a mostly-casual corpus are disproportionately likely
to be casual examples that happen to be semantically close in content but carry a different register label.
This distinction matters for where future effort should go – it argues for a larger, more balanced corpus
before it argues for a different embedding model or a reranking stage, and it is a reminder that register-
label agreement is a proxy for retrieval quality, not a definition of it.

    Neither of these interpretations, however, is something this evaluation can confirm. Both are offered
as explanations consistent with the observed patterns, not as conclusions the current evidence
establishes; distinguishing them from alternative explanations is exactly what the baseline- and human-
evaluation studies proposed in Section IV-F below are designed to do.

    Beyond the evaluation itself, the practical case for the retrieval-based approach rests on what it
avoids: no fine-tuning run, no annotated training set beyond the retrieval corpus itself, and a fully offline
deployment (Ollama, ChromaDB, and the embedding model all run locally) that keeps user text off any
external server. That offline property is a genuine advantage independent of how the quality comparison
against fine-tuning eventually turns out, since it is a deployment-cost and privacy argument rather than
a quality argument.

F. Limitations
   We see the following as the paper’s most significant limitations, in roughly descending order of
how much they constrain the conclusions that can currently be drawn:

  • No baseline comparison. The paper does not currently include the controlled comparison its central
     claim requires: (1) the Llama 3.1 8B model prompted with no retrieved examples, as a baseline;
     (2) the full LLM+RAG pipeline as deployed; and (3) a comparison across retrieval depths, such
     as top-1 versus the deployed default of top-4, to test whether the amount of retrieved context
     affects output quality. Every claim in this paper about what retrieval contributes is currently
     supported only by the qualitative pattern in Section IV-C and the architectural reasoning in the
     Discussion above, not by this comparison. This is the single most important gap and the first item
     in our future work.
  • Retrieval evaluation does not confirm exclusion of self-retrieval. The Precision@k/Recall@k
    statistics in Fig. 4(c) were not confirmed to use a query set held out from the indexed corpus. If
    any queried example is also indexed in ChromaDB, it can retrieve itself as a trivial, artificially
    perfect match, which would inflate the reported precision relative to a genuinely unseen input.
    This must be corrected – by re-running the evaluation with an explicit held-out split – before these
    numbers can be interpreted as a measure of generalization.
  • No style filtering or re-ranking is implemented. The retrieval module (Section III) performs a single
     embedding-similarity search and returns the top-k nearest neighbors; it does not filter candidates
     by register metadata and does not apply a separate re-ranking model. Any register agreement
     observed between a query and its retrieved examples (Fig. 4c) is an emergent side effect of
     semantic similarity, not the result of an explicit design constraint. We note this explicitly because
     both capabilities are easy to assume from the RAG literature generally; in the system described
     here, they are candidate future extensions, not existing components.
  • No human evaluation. Naturalness and fluency – arguably the paper’s central claims – are currently
     assessed only through the authors’ own qualitative reading of five illustrative examples, not
     through independent human raters.
  • No semantic-preservation metric. ROUGE and the length-ratio statistics indicate that rewriting
    occurred and that it did not drastically shorten or lengthen the input, but neither directly measures
    whether the rewritten meaning, facts, or figures match the original; a low-overlap output that also
    altered a fact or a number would look identical, by these metrics, to a successful humanization.
  • Dataset class imbalance. Casual and formal_to_casual dominate the register-metadata distribution
    of the 3,173-pair corpus (Fig. 4a–b); results and retrieval quality for underrepresented registers
    such as humorous or calm should be read with correspondingly less confidence.
  • Single LLM, single embedding model. All results reflect one specific pairing (Llama 3.1 8B and a
     multilingual MiniLM embedding model) at a fixed retrieval depth (k = 4); neither the generation
     quality nor the retrieval quality has been tested against alternative model choices or retrieval
     depths, so it is not yet known how much of the observed behavior is specific to this configuration.
  • No comparison against a fine-tuned baseline. The paper argues for retrieval over fine-tuning on
    cost and deployment grounds, but does not include a quality comparison against a fine-tuned
    Persian style-transfer model such as Fa-BERT2BERT [6] run on the same data, so the quality side
    of that trade-off remains unquantified. This comparison would also only be partially informative,




                                                   18
     since Fa-BERT2BERT targets formality style transfer specifically rather than the broader
     humanization objective of this paper.
    None of these limitations is fatal to the paper’s central contribution – a working, fine-tuning-free
architecture and a dataset and evaluation methodology to study it – but they do mean the paper’s
research claims should currently be read as preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than
confirmatory.


                                           V. Conclusion
     This paper set out to answer a narrow question: whether retrieval-augmented, in-context prompting
can make AI-generated Persian text read as natural, human-written text without fine-tuning the
underlying model, while preserving its meaning and its original register. We built a working system to
test that question – a modular RAG pipeline combining multilingual sentence embeddings, ChromaDB,
LangGraph, FastAPI, and a locally deployed Llama 3.1 8B model – and a 3,173-pair Persian parallel
corpus, register-labeled for analysis, to drive and characterize it.

    What we can say with reasonable confidence: the retrieval module returns register-consistent
examples in roughly half of top-ranked cases (with the important caveat that self-retrieval has not been
ruled out), the generated text diverges substantially in wording from its input (the low-ROUGE result,
read the way Falakaflaki and Shamsfard’s critique of overlap metrics suggests it should be read [6]),
and stays close to it in length. Qualitative inspection of five hand-picked examples shows the kind of
edits one would hope for – reduced AI-typical stiffness, simplified constructions, numbers and
obligations preserved exactly – without the register of the input being changed.

    What we cannot yet say, and have tried not to overstate, is whether retrieval is the reason for any
of this rather than the underlying LLM alone, since that comparison has not yet been run (Section IV-
F). We see that as the paper’s honest bottom line: a functioning, low-cost, privacy-preserving
architecture with encouraging preliminary signal, and a clearly scoped set of experiments – not further
engineering – standing between here and a confirmed research result.

A. Future Work
     The most direct continuation of this work is not a new component but a missing comparison, and
we specify it precisely: (1) the Llama 3.1 8B model with retrieval disabled, as a baseline; (2) the same
model with the full RAG pipeline, as deployed; and (3) a comparison across retrieval depths,
specifically top-1 versus the deployed default of top-4, to check whether retrieving more examples
actually helps. Alongside that comparison, a held-out evaluation split – separating the examples used
to build the ChromaDB index from the examples used to query it during evaluation – would close the
self-retrieval gap noted in Section IV-F. A small human evaluation of naturalness, meaning
preservation, and register match, even on a few dozen examples with two or three Persian/Dari-fluent
raters, would give the naturalness claims a footing beyond the authors’ own reading of five examples.

     Beyond that closer-to-term work, several extensions would strengthen the system itself: expanding
the dataset with additional registers and domain-specific examples to correct the class imbalance
documented in Section IV-C; adding a register-aware filtering step or a re-ranking stage to the retrieval
module – neither of which exists in the current single-pass similarity search – once the baseline
experiment above establishes whether retrieval quality is actually the bottleneck; adding BLEU [25]
and an embedding-based semantic-similarity score alongside ROUGE, to directly measure meaning and
fact preservation rather than inferring it from low lexical overlap; and a direct quantitative comparison



                                                   19
against a fine-tuned Persian style-transfer baseline such as Fa-BERT2BERT [6], understanding that its
narrower formality-transfer objective makes the comparison only partially informative for the broader
humanization task studied here. Longer-term, packaging the system as a hosted service would be a
natural next step once the underlying evaluation questions above are resolved.
